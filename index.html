Unit 1Atomic Structure & Properties

Moles & Molar Mass

moles = mass / molar massfundamental conversion
  • Molarity = mol solute / L solution
  • Empirical = smallest ratio; Molecular = actual
  • Formula unit = smallest collection of atoms making a compound

Mass Spectroscopy

  • e⁻ = 0 amu, proton = 1 amu, neutron = 1 amu; atomic # = protons
  • Isotopes = same element, different neutrons
  • % abundance: x(m₁) + (1−x)(m₂) = avg atomic mass
  • y-axis = relative abundance, x-axis = mass # → avg atomic mass

Electron Configuration

Fₑ = kq₁q₂ / r²Coulomb's Law
  • Aufbau → fill increasing energy; Pauli → 2e⁻/orbital opposite spins; Hund's → half-fill first
  • Exceptions: Cr, Mo, Cu, Ag (half/full d more stable)
  • Valence = s & p; Core = d & f

Periodic Trends

  • Atomic radius: ↑ down group, ↓ across period
  • IE: ↓ down, ↑ across (Group 2A, 5A exceptions)
  • Electronegativity: same as IE; larger nuclear charge + smaller atom = higher
  • Ionic radius: cations smaller (+), anions larger (−)

PES

  • E_photon = hν; photoelectric effect → enough energy emits 1 e⁻
  • PES → distinguishes orbital energy levels; peak height = # of e⁻ in subshell
  • Valence e⁻: low binding energy; Core e⁻: high binding energy
Unit 2Chemical Bonds

Bond Types

  • Ionic: large ΔEN, metal+nonmetal, transfers e⁻
  • Covalent: shares e⁻, nonmetal+nonmetal (polar or nonpolar)
  • Metallic: sea of e⁻, good conductor, malleable

Lewis Structures & Formal Charge

  • FC = VE − nonbonding e⁻ − ½(bonding e⁻)
  • FC closest to 0 = most stable; draw ALL resonance structures
  • Resonance → delocalized e⁻, more stable

Bond Properties

  • Single=1σ; Double=1σ+1π; Triple=1σ+2π
  • Higher bond order → shorter + stronger
  • Lone pairs repel more → compress bond angles

Crystalline Solids

  • Ionic: high MP, brittle, conducts when molten/dissolved
  • Metallic: conducts as solid, malleable
  • Covalent network: very high MP, poor conductor (diamond, SiO₂)
  • Molecular: low MP, poor conductor, held by IMFs
Unit 3IMFs & Properties of Matter

IMFs (weakest → strongest)

  • LDF: all molecules; larger molecule = stronger
  • Dipole-Dipole: polar molecules only
  • H-Bonding: N, O, or F bonded to H
  • Ion-Dipole: ion + polar molecule; strongest
  • Stronger IMF → higher BP, viscosity, surface tension

Ideal Gas Law

PV = nRTR = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K; T in Kelvin
P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂Combined Gas Law
  • STP: 273 K, 1 atm → 22.4 L/mol
  • Dalton's Law: P_total = P₁ + P₂ + …; Pₐ = Xₐ × P_total

Kinetic Molecular Theory

  • Constant random motion, elastic collisions, no IMFs (ideal)
  • KE_avg = (3/2)RT → proportional to T only
  • Maxwell-Boltzmann: higher T → curve shifts right, flattens
  • Lighter molecule → faster; heavier → slower (effusion/diffusion)

Real Gas Deviations

  • High P → molecular volume matters → real V > ideal V
  • Low T → attractions significant → real P < ideal P
  • van der Waals equation corrects for both
Unit 4Chemical Reactions

Reaction Types

  • Synthesis: A+B→AB; Decomposition: AB→A+B
  • Single/Double Replacement; Combustion: CₓHᵧ+O₂→CO₂+H₂O
  • Precipitation, Acid-Base (proton), Redox (electron)

Stoichiometry & Yield

  • Mole ratio from balanced equation
  • Limiting reagent determines theoretical yield
  • % yield = (actual / theoretical) × 100
  • Moles = M × L for solutions

Net Ionic Equations

  • Break strong acids, bases, soluble salts into ions
  • Weak acids/bases stay molecular
  • Cancel spectator ions → net ionic equation

Redox & Balancing

  • OIL RIG / LEO says GER (e⁻ transfer)
  • Acidic: balance O w/ H₂O, H w/ H⁺, charge w/ e⁻
  • Basic: do acidic first, then add OH⁻ to both sides
Ox # rules: O=−2, H=+1, sum neutral=0, sum ion=charge

Titration

MₐVₐ = M_bV_bat equivalence point
  • Equivalence point: mol acid = mol base
  • Endpoint ≈ equivalence point (indicator color change)

Rate Laws

Collision Theory & Arrhenius

k = Ae^(−Ea/RT)Arrhenius equation
  • Collision needs: energy ≥ Eₐ AND correct orientation
  • ↑ T, ↑ conc, ↑ surface area, catalyst → all increase rate
  • Catalyst → lowers Eₐ equally for forward and reverse

Mechanisms

  • Rate-determining step = slowest → dictates overall rate law
  • Intermediate: produced then consumed, NOT in overall equation
  • Catalyst: appears early, regenerated later, NOT in overall equation
  • Proposed mechanism rate law must match experimental

Energy Profiles

  • Peak = transition state; Valley = intermediate
  • ΔH = energy(products) − energy(reactants)
  • Catalyst: lowers BOTH Eₐ(fwd) and Eₐ(rev) equally; ΔH unchanged
Unit 6Thermodynamics

Heat & Calorimetry

q = mcΔTc(water) = 4.184 J/g·°C; q_rxn = −q_solution
  • Endothermic: +q_rxn; Exothermic: −q_rxn
  • Coffee cup calorimeter = constant P → ΔH; Bomb = constant V
  • Bond breaking = endo; Bond forming = exo

Enthalpy & Hess's Law

ΔH°rxn = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)
ΔH°rxn = Σ(BE broken) − Σ(BE formed)
  • ΔH°f elements in standard state = 0
  • Reverse → flip sign; multiply → multiply ΔH; add → add ΔH

Entropy & Gibbs Free Energy

ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° = −RT ln K = −nFE°
  • ΔG < 0 → spontaneous; ΔG = 0 → equilibrium; ΔG > 0 → nonspontaneous
  • +ΔS: gas > liquid > solid; more moles of gas product

Unit 7Equilibrium

Equilibrium Constant

Kc = [C]^c[D]^d / [A]^a[B]^b
  • K changes only with temperature; solids & pure liquids excluded
  • K>>1 → products favored; K<<1 → reactants favored
  • Dynamic: forward rate = reverse rate (still occurring)

Q vs K & ICE Tables

  • Q < K → shift right; Q > K → shift left; Q = K → equilibrium
  • ICE: I=initial, C=±x × stoich coeff, E=equilibrium
  • Drop x when x/[A]₀ < 5% (small x approx.)

Le Châtelier's Principle

  • ↑ reactant → right; ↑ product → left
  • ↑ T: exo → left; endo → right
  • ↑ P / ↓ V → fewer moles of gas side
  • Catalyst → no shift; Inert gas at const V → no effect

Ksp & Common Ion Effect

Ksp = [cation]^x[anion]^y
  • Concentrations start at 0 in ICE; use coefficients as exponents AND x-multipliers
  • Common ion → shifts left → suppresses solubility
  • Acidic solution → ↑ solubility of basic salts

Unit 8Acid-Base Equilibria

pH & pOH

pH=−log[H₃O⁺] pOH=−log[OH⁻] pH+pOH=14
Kw = 1×10⁻¹⁴ @ 25°C
  • Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₃, HClO₄
  • Strong bases: NaOH, KOH, LiOH, Ca(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂

Ka, Kb & Conjugate Pairs

Ka=[H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA] Kb=[OH⁻][HB⁺]/[B]
Ka × Kb = Kw = 1×10⁻¹⁴conjugate pairs
  • pKa = −log Ka; smaller pKa → stronger acid
  • % ionization = [H⁺]eq / [HA]₀ × 100

Buffers & Henderson-Hasselbalch

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])
  • Buffer = weak acid + conjugate base; resists pH change
  • Max buffering: pH = pKa, [HA] = [A⁻] (half-equivalence point)
  • More conj. base → ↑ capacity for acid; More conj. acid → ↑ for base

Titration Types

  • SA + SB → just stoich (BCA)
  • WA + SB → BCA → ICE → Henderson-Hasselbalch
  • WB + SA → BCA → ICE → H-H with Kb
  • Equiv point: MₐVₐ = M_bV_b; half-equiv: pH = pKa
  • Indicator: pKa ≈ pH at equivalence point ± 1

Acid Strength & Structure

  • Stronger H-X bond → weaker acid; HF < HCl < HBr < HI
  • More oxygens in oxyacid → stronger acid (more EN = weaker O-H)
  • Resonance stabilization of conjugate base → stronger acid
Unit 9Electrochemistry

Galvanic Cells

  • Spontaneous redox → electrical energy
  • Anode = oxidation (−); Cathode = reduction (+) → Red Cat, An Ox
  • e⁻ flow: anode → wire → cathode; salt bridge maintains neutrality

Cell Potential

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
  • E°cell > 0 → spontaneous; intensive property (don't multiply by n)
  • More positive reduction potential → stronger oxidizing agent

ΔG°, K, and E°cell

ΔG° = −nFE°cell = −RT ln KF = 96,485 C/mol
  • Positive E° ↔ negative ΔG° ↔ K > 1 → all = spontaneous

Nernst Equation

Ecell = E°cell − (0.0592/n) log Q @ 25°C
  • Use for non-standard conditions (conc ≠ 1 M)
  • At equilibrium: Ecell = 0, Q = K

Electrolytic Cells & Faraday

mol e⁻ = (A × s) / 964851 A = 1 C/s
  • Non-spontaneous → requires external electrical energy
  • Anode = oxidation (+), Cathode = reduction (−) ← signs flip vs galvanic
  • Used for electroplating, water electrolysis, metal purification

1
Electron Configuration
Unit 1
▾
Example Question Write the ground-state electron configuration for Fe (Z=26) and Fe²⁺. Identify the number of unpaired electrons in each.
1
Fill orbitals in Aufbau order

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p…

Fe (26e⁻): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d⁶
2
Apply Hund's Rule to 3d⁶

Fill each of the 5 d-orbitals with one electron first (half-fill), then pair. 3d⁶ = ↑↓ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ → 4 unpaired electrons.

3
Form the ion — remove from highest n first

Fe²⁺ loses 2 electrons from 4s first (not 3d).

Fe²⁺: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶3d⁶

3d⁶ still has 4 unpaired electrons.

FRQ tip: Always remove electrons from the highest principal quantum number first when forming cations. A common trap is removing from 3d instead of 4s.
Answer

Fe: [Ar]4s²3d⁶ — 4 unpaired e⁻. Fe²⁺: [Ar]3d⁶ — 4 unpaired e⁻. (Both have the same number here, but this is often different.)

Periodic Trends Comparison
Unit 1
▾
Example Question Rank Na, Mg, and Al in order of increasing first ionization energy. Explain.
1
Identify position on periodic table

All three are in Period 3. Going left to right: Na (Group 1) → Mg (Group 2) → Al (Group 13).

2
Apply the trend — but note the exception

IE generally increases across a period due to increasing nuclear charge with the same shielding. However, Al is a Group 2A→3A exception: Al's 3p electron is easier to remove than Mg's 3s because 3p is higher energy and gets more shielding from 3s.

Na < Al < Mg
FRQ tip: Always explain the exception — "Al's 3p electron is higher energy and more shielded than Mg's full 3s subshell, making it easier to remove despite Al having higher nuclear charge."
3
VSEPR & Hybridization
Unit 2
▾
Example Question Determine the molecular geometry, bond angle, and hybridization of SF₄.
1
Count electron domains on central atom

S has 6 valence electrons. 4 F atoms each form 1 bond. That's 4 bonding pairs + 1 lone pair on S = 5 electron domains.

2
Electron geometry vs molecular geometry

5 domains → electron geometry = trigonal bipyramidal (sp³d). With 1 lone pair, molecular geometry = see-saw.

Hybridization: sp³d
3
Lone pair placement rule

In trigonal bipyramidal, lone pairs go in equatorial positions (less repulsion with 90° neighbors). Bond angles: ~90° and ~120° (compressed slightly by LP repulsion).

FRQ tip: Always state both electron geometry AND molecular geometry. Graders need both. Also explain why lone pairs go equatorial for 5-domain structures.
Answer

Molecular geometry: see-saw. Hybridization: sp³d. Bond angles: ~90°/~120° (compressed by lone pair repulsion). Electron geometry: trigonal bipyramidal.

Ideal Gas Law Calculation
Unit 3
▾
Example Question A 3.50 g sample of N₂ gas is held in a 2.00 L container at 25°C. What is the pressure in atm?
1
Convert all units

T = 25°C + 273 = 298 K. Molar mass N₂ = 28 g/mol.

n = 3.50 g ÷ 28.0 g/mol = 0.125 mol
2
Plug into PV = nRT, solve for P
P = nRT/V = (0.125)(0.0821)(298) / 2.00 P = 1.53 atm
⚠ Always convert °C to K. Forgetting this is the #1 gas law mistake.
5
IMF Ranking by Boiling Point
Unit 3
▾
Example Question Rank CH₄, HF, and H₂O in order of increasing boiling point. Explain in terms of intermolecular forces.
1
Identify the IMF type for each

CH₄: nonpolar, only LDF. HF: polar with H bonded to F → hydrogen bonding. H₂O: polar with H bonded to O → hydrogen bonding AND can form more H-bonds per molecule (2 donors, 2 acceptors).

2
Rank by IMF strength

LDF only (weakest) < H-bonding. Between HF and H₂O: H₂O has a higher BP despite lower molar mass because each molecule can form more hydrogen bonds (H₂O has 2 H-bond donors AND 2 acceptors; HF has 1 donor, 4 acceptors but network is less extensive).

CH₄ < HF < H₂O
FRQ tip: Never just say "stronger IMFs." Name the specific IMF type and explain WHY it exists (what structural feature causes it). Saying "H₂O has hydrogen bonding because H is bonded directly to O" earns the point. Saying "stronger forces" does not.

Stoichiometry + Limiting Reagent
Unit 4
▾
Example Question 12.0 g of Fe reacts with 8.00 g of S to form FeS. Which is the limiting reagent? How many grams of FeS are produced?
1
Write and balance the equation
Fe + S → FeS (1:1:1 ratio)
2
Convert both reactants to moles
mol Fe = 12.0 / 55.8 = 0.215 mol mol S = 8.00 / 32.1 = 0.249 mol
3
Find the limiting reagent

1:1 ratio → whichever has fewer moles is limiting. Fe (0.215 mol) < S (0.249 mol) → Fe is limiting.

4
Calculate product using limiting reagent
mol FeS = 0.215 mol (1:1 ratio with Fe) mass FeS = 0.215 × 87.9 g/mol = 18.9 g
7
Balancing Redox in Acidic Solution
Unit 4
▾
Example Question Balance in acidic solution: MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺
1
Split into half-reactions
Reduction: MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺
2
Balance O with H₂O, H with H⁺
MnO₄⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (balance O) MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (balance H)
3
Balance charge with electrons
MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O (left = +2, right = +2 ✓) Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + 1e⁻
4
Equalize electrons and add

Multiply oxidation half by 5 to match 5e⁻:

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O + 5Fe³⁺
FRQ tip: Always verify: atoms balanced ✓, charge balanced ✓. State which species is oxidized and which is reduced. The oxidizing agent is the one that gets reduced (MnO₄⁻ here).

Net Ionic Equation
Unit 4
▾
Example Question Write the net ionic equation for: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq)
1
Identify what stays molecular vs dissociates

PbI₂ is insoluble (s) → stays as PbI₂. All others are soluble salts → dissociate into ions.

Pb²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻ + 2K⁺ + 2I⁻ → PbI₂(s) + 2K⁺ + 2NO₃⁻
2
Cancel spectator ions

K⁺ and NO₃⁻ appear identically on both sides → cancel.

Pb²⁺(aq) + 2I⁻(aq) → PbI₂(s)
FRQ tip: Include state symbols (aq), (s), (l), (g) — graders check for these. Also: weak acids/bases like CH₃COOH stay molecular even in solution.

Determining Rate Law from Experimental Data
Unit 5
▾
Example Question Given data: Exp 1: [A]=0.10, [B]=0.10, rate=2.0×10⁻³ M/s. Exp 2: [A]=0.20, [B]=0.10, rate=8.0×10⁻³ M/s. Exp 3: [A]=0.10, [B]=0.20, rate=2.0×10⁻³ M/s. Find the rate law and k.
1
Find order with respect to A (hold B constant)

Compare Exp 1 and 2: [A] doubles (×2), rate quadruples (×4). So rate ∝ [A]².

rate₂/rate₁ = ([A]₂/[A]₁)^m → 4 = 2^m → m = 2
2
Find order with respect to B (hold A constant)

Compare Exp 1 and 3: [B] doubles, rate unchanged (×1). Rate is zero order in B.

rate₃/rate₁ = ([B]₃/[B]₁)^n → 1 = 2^n → n = 0
3
Write rate law and solve for k
rate = k[A]² k = rate/[A]² = 2.0×10⁻³ / (0.10)² = 0.20 M⁻¹s⁻¹ rate = 0.20[A]² ; overall order = 2nd
⚠ Orders come from experiment ONLY — never from the balanced equation coefficients.

Integrated Rate Law & Half-life
Unit 5
▾
Example Question A first-order reaction has k = 0.0250 s⁻¹. If [A]₀ = 0.800 M, what is [A] after 45.0 s? What is the half-life?
1
Use the first-order integrated rate law
ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt ln[A] = ln(0.800) − (0.0250)(45.0) = −0.223 − 1.125 = −1.348 [A] = e^(−1.348) = 0.259 M
2
Calculate half-life
t½ = 0.693 / k = 0.693 / 0.0250 = 27.7 s

Note: first-order half-life is constant — it doesn't depend on concentration.

FRQ tip: If the problem says half-life is constant, the reaction is first order. If half-life changes with concentration, it's zero or second order. This is a common FRQ identification question.

Reaction Mechanisms
Unit 5
▾
Example Question A reaction has mechanism: Step 1 (slow): NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO. Step 2 (fast): NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂. (a) Write the overall equation. (b) Identify intermediates. (c) Write the rate law.
1
(a) Overall equation — add steps, cancel intermediates
NO₂ + NO₂ + NO₃ + CO → NO₃ + NO + NO₂ + CO₂

Cancel NO₃ (appears on both sides):

NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂
2
(b) Identify intermediates

NO₃ is produced in Step 1 and consumed in Step 2 → it's an intermediate (not in the overall equation).

3
(c) Rate law comes from the slow (rate-determining) step
Slow step: NO₂ + NO₂ → products rate = k[NO₂]²
FRQ tip: Rate law comes directly from the slow step's reactants. If the slow step uses an intermediate, you must express it in terms of the original reactants using the fast equilibrium step before it.

Hess's Law / ΔH°rxn
Unit 6
▾
Example Question Given: (1) C + O₂ → CO₂, ΔH = −393 kJ. (2) H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O, ΔH = −286 kJ. (3) CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O, ΔH = −890 kJ. Find ΔH for: C + 2H₂ → CH₄
1
Target equation: C + 2H₂ → CH₄. Work backward from what you need.

Need C on left → use rxn (1) as-is: C + O₂ → CO₂, ΔH₁ = −393 kJ

Need 2H₂ on left → use rxn (2) ×2: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ΔH = 2(−286) = −572 kJ

Need CH₄ on right → reverse rxn (3): CO₂ + 2H₂O → CH₄ + 2O₂, ΔH = +890 kJ

2
Add all three and cancel intermediates

CO₂, H₂O, and O₂ all cancel.

ΔH = −393 + (−572) + 890 = −75 kJ
FRQ tip: Show your work clearly — write each modified equation, show which you flipped or multiplied, then add. Graders follow your logic step by step.
13
Gibbs Free Energy & Spontaneity
Unit 6
▾
Example Question A reaction has ΔH° = +120 kJ/mol and ΔS° = +250 J/mol·K. (a) Is the reaction spontaneous at 25°C? (b) At what temperature does it become spontaneous?
1
(a) Calculate ΔG° at 25°C (298 K)
⚠ Convert ΔS° to kJ: 250 J/mol·K = 0.250 kJ/mol·K
ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° = 120 − (298)(0.250) = 120 − 74.5 = +45.5 kJ

ΔG° > 0 → not spontaneous at 25°C.

2
(b) Find crossover temperature where ΔG° = 0
0 = ΔH° − TΔS° → T = ΔH°/ΔS° T = 120 kJ / 0.250 kJ/K = 480 K (207°C)

Above 480 K, TΔS° dominates → ΔG° < 0 → spontaneous.

FRQ tip: The #1 error is mismatched units — ΔH in kJ but ΔS in J. Always convert ΔS to kJ/K before plugging in.

Calorimetry Calculation
Unit 6
▾
Example Question When 0.500 g of a solid dissolves in 50.0 g of water in a coffee cup calorimeter, the temperature rises from 22.4°C to 25.1°C. Calculate ΔH_dissolution in kJ/mol. (MM of solid = 80.0 g/mol)
1
Find q_solution
q = mcΔT = (50.0 g)(4.184 J/g·°C)(25.1 − 22.4) = (50.0)(4.184)(2.7) q_solution = 564.8 J = 0.5648 kJ
2
q_rxn = −q_solution

Temperature rose → solution absorbed heat → rxn released heat → exothermic.

q_rxn = −0.5648 kJ
3
Convert to kJ/mol
mol solid = 0.500 g / 80.0 g/mol = 6.25×10⁻³ mol ΔH = −0.5648 / 6.25×10⁻³ = −90.4 kJ/mol

ICE Table + Equilibrium Concentrations
Unit 7
▾
Example Question For N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), Kc = 0.500 at 400°C. If [N₂]₀ = 1.00 M and [H₂]₀ = 1.50 M, find equilibrium concentrations.
1
Set up ICE table
N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ I: 1.00 1.50 0 C: −x −3x +2x E: 1−x 1.5−3x 2x
2
Substitute into K expression
Kc = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³) = (2x)² / ((1−x)(1.5−3x)³) = 0.500

This cubic is complex — check if small-x approximation applies: K = 0.500 is not tiny, so you may need to solve numerically or use approximation with check. For AP, set up the expression and solve or approximate.

FRQ tip: On the AP exam, K values are often chosen so that small-x works or the algebra simplifies. Always check: if x / initial < 5%, approximation is valid. If not, solve the full expression. You will not lose points for using the quadratic if you show work.
16
Le Châtelier Prediction (FRQ style)
Unit 7
▾
Example Question For 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), ΔH = −198 kJ. Predict the effect on [SO₃] if (a) temperature is increased, (b) pressure is decreased, (c) a catalyst is added.
1
(a) Increase temperature

Reaction is exothermic (ΔH < 0). Treat heat as a product. Adding heat (↑T) → shifts LEFT → [SO₃] decreases.

FRQ wording: "Increasing temperature shifts the equilibrium to the left because the forward reaction is exothermic; the system opposes the added heat by favoring the reverse reaction, decreasing [SO₃]."
2
(b) Decrease pressure (increase volume)

Left side: 2 + 1 = 3 moles gas. Right side: 2 moles gas. Decreasing pressure → system shifts toward more moles of gas → shifts LEFT → [SO₃] decreases.

3
(c) Catalyst added

Catalyst lowers Eₐ equally for forward and reverse. Equilibrium position does NOT shift. [SO₃] is unchanged (equilibrium reached faster, but same concentrations).

FRQ tip: Every answer needs a BECAUSE. "Shifts left" = 0 points. "Shifts left because the reaction is exothermic and increasing temperature favors the endothermic (reverse) reaction" = full points.
17
Ksp + Common Ion Effect
Unit 7
▾
Example Question Ksp of PbCl₂ = 1.6×10⁻⁵. (a) Find molar solubility in pure water. (b) Find molar solubility in 0.10 M NaCl.
1
(a) Solubility in pure water — ICE table
PbCl₂ ⇌ Pb²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ I: solid 0 0 C: +x +2x E: x 2x Ksp = [Pb²⁺][Cl⁻]² = (x)(2x)² = 4x³ = 1.6×10⁻⁵ x³ = 4.0×10⁻⁶ → x = 0.0159 M
2
(b) In 0.10 M NaCl — common ion Cl⁻ already present
I: 0 0.10 C: +x +2x E: x 0.10+2x ≈ 0.10 (small x approx.) Ksp = (x)(0.10)² = 0.010x = 1.6×10⁻⁵ x = 1.6×10⁻³ M (100× less soluble than pure water!)

The common ion (Cl⁻) suppresses the solubility by shifting equilibrium left.

18
Weak Acid pH (ICE Table)
Unit 8
▾
Example Question Find the pH of 0.150 M acetic acid (CH₃COOH). Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵.
1
Write the equilibrium and ICE table
CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ I: 0.150 0 0 C: −x +x +x E: 0.150−x x x
2
Substitute into Ka — use small-x approx.
Ka = x² / (0.150 − x) ≈ x² / 0.150 = 1.8×10⁻⁵ x² = (1.8×10⁻⁵)(0.150) = 2.7×10⁻⁶ x = [H⁺] = 1.64×10⁻³ M

Check: 1.64×10⁻³ / 0.150 = 1.1% < 5% ✓ approximation valid

3
Calculate pH
pH = −log(1.64×10⁻³) = 2.79
FRQ tip: Always check the 5% rule. If it fails, use the quadratic formula. Show ALL intermediate steps — graders award partial credit for correct setup even if arithmetic is wrong.
19
Buffer pH (Henderson-Hasselbalch)
Unit 8
▾
Example Question A buffer is made with 0.200 mol CH₃COOH and 0.150 mol CH₃COONa in 1.00 L. Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵. (a) Find pH. (b) Find pH after adding 0.050 mol HCl.
1
(a) Henderson-Hasselbalch directly
pKa = −log(1.8×10⁻⁵) = 4.74 pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) = 4.74 + log(0.150/0.200) pH = 4.74 + (−0.125) = 4.62
2
(b) Adding acid — BCA table first

HCl is a strong acid → reacts completely with conjugate base (CH₃COO⁻).

CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH Before: 0.150 mol A⁻, 0.200 mol HA, + 0.050 mol H⁺ After: 0.100 mol A⁻, 0.250 mol HA, 0 mol H⁺
3
Apply Henderson-Hasselbalch with new amounts
pH = 4.74 + log(0.100/0.250) pH = 4.74 + (−0.398) = 4.34
FRQ tip: BCA = Before, Change, After. Run BCA first for any strong acid/base addition to a buffer. Then apply H-H to the new amounts. You can use moles directly in H-H (volume cancels).
20
Titration Curve Problem
Unit 8
▾
Example Question 25.0 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid (Ka=1.8×10⁻⁵) is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH. Find pH at: (a) start, (b) half-equivalence point (12.5 mL NaOH added), (c) equivalence point (25.0 mL NaOH added).
1
(a) Start — weak acid only

Use ICE table with Ka.

x² / 0.100 = 1.8×10⁻⁵ → x = 1.34×10⁻³ M pH = 2.87
2
(b) Half-equivalence point

Half the acid has been neutralized → [HA] = [A⁻]. H-H equation: pH = pKa + log(1) = pKa.

pH = pKa = 4.74

This is the key shortcut — at half-equivalence, pH = pKa always.

3
(c) Equivalence point — all HA converted to A⁻

Now you have 0.00250 mol CH₃COO⁻ in 50.0 mL = 0.0500 M. This is a weak base — use Kb.

Kb = Kw/Ka = 1×10⁻¹⁴ / 1.8×10⁻⁵ = 5.56×10⁻¹⁰ x² / 0.0500 = 5.56×10⁻¹⁰ → x = [OH⁻] = 5.27×10⁻⁶ pOH = 5.28 → pH = 14 − 5.28 = 8.72 pH = 8.72 (basic at equivalence point — expected for WA/SB titration)
FRQ tip: For WA + SB titrations, the equivalence point pH is always ABOVE 7 because you're left with the conjugate base of the weak acid, which is basic. Graders expect you to state this explicitly.
21
Galvanic Cell + E°cell
Unit 9
▾
Example Question Given: Zn²⁺/Zn E° = −0.76 V; Cu²⁺/Cu E° = +0.34 V. (a) Write the cell reaction. (b) Calculate E°cell. (c) Is it spontaneous?
1
(a) Identify cathode and anode

More positive E° is reduced at cathode: Cu²⁺/Cu (+0.34 V) → cathode. Less positive is oxidized at anode: Zn/Zn²⁺ (−0.76 V) → anode.

Anode (oxidation): Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ Cathode (reduction): Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu Overall: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu
2
(b) Calculate E°cell
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V
⚠ Do NOT multiply E° by n when balancing. E° is an intensive property.
3
(c) Spontaneity

E°cell = +1.10 V > 0 → spontaneous. This is consistent with ΔG° = −nFE° = negative.

FRQ tip: Always identify anode and cathode explicitly. State direction of electron flow (anode → cathode through wire). State direction of ion migration in the salt bridge. These are separate point-earning statements.

ΔG°, K, and E° Connection
Unit 9
▾
Example Question For the Zn/Cu cell above (E°cell = +1.10 V, n = 2), calculate ΔG° and K at 25°C.
1
Calculate ΔG°
ΔG° = −nFE° = −(2)(96485)(1.10) ΔG° = −2.12×10⁵ J = −212 kJ/mol

Negative → spontaneous (confirms E° > 0).

2
Calculate K
ΔG° = −RT ln K → ln K = −ΔG°/RT = 212000 / (8.314)(298) ln K = 85.6 → K = e^85.6 ≈ 1.6×10³⁷ K = 1.6×10³⁷ (products hugely favored)
FRQ tip: You can also use E°cell = (0.0592/n) log K directly. Both give the same answer. Know both routes.
23
Nernst Equation (Non-Standard Conditions)
Unit 9
▾
Example Question For Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, E°cell = +1.10 V, n = 2. Calculate Ecell when [Zn²⁺] = 2.00 M and [Cu²⁺] = 0.100 M at 25°C.
1
Write Q for the cell reaction
Q = [Zn²⁺] / [Cu²⁺] = 2.00 / 0.100 = 20.0

Solids (Zn, Cu) are not included in Q.

2
Apply Nernst equation
Ecell = E°cell − (0.0592/n) log Q Ecell = 1.10 − (0.0592/2) log(20.0) Ecell = 1.10 − (0.0296)(1.301) Ecell = 1.10 − 0.0385 = 1.06 V

Q > 1 → Ecell < E°cell (as expected: concentrations favor reactants less).

FRQ tip: Use log (base 10) with the 0.0592 version at 25°C. Use ln with the full RT/nF version at other temperatures. Make sure Q uses the cell reaction (not a random equation).

Electrolysis / Faraday's Law
Unit 9
▾
Example Question A current of 2.50 A is passed through a solution of CuSO₄ for 45.0 minutes. How many grams of Cu are deposited at the cathode?
1
Convert time to seconds, find total charge
t = 45.0 min × 60 s/min = 2700 s charge = I × t = 2.50 A × 2700 s = 6750 C
2
Convert charge to moles of electrons
mol e⁻ = 6750 C / 96485 C/mol = 0.06995 mol
3
Convert mol e⁻ to mol Cu using half-reaction
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (2 mol e⁻ per mol Cu) mol Cu = 0.06995 / 2 = 0.03498 mol mass Cu = 0.03498 × 63.5 g/mol = 2.22 g
FRQ tip: Chain: A × s → coulombs → mol e⁻ → mol product (via half-reaction ratio) → grams. Write every step. This problem type shows up almost every year.

FRQ Justification Framework
All Units
▾
How to earn every point on the FRQ This is the single most important section. Most 4s become 5s by writing better justifications, not by knowing more content.
1
The CLAIM → EVIDENCE → REASONING structure

Every FRQ answer needs all three parts:

CLAIM: What happens (the answer) EVIDENCE: The data/formula/value that supports it REASONING: WHY that evidence leads to that claim
Bad answer (0 pts): "The rate increases."

Good answer (full pts): "The rate increases because increasing temperature increases the fraction of molecules with kinetic energy greater than or equal to the activation energy Ea, as described by the Arrhenius equation k = Ae^(−Ea/RT). More successful collisions per unit time means a higher rate."
2
Justification templates by topic

Le Châtelier: "The equilibrium shifts [direction] because [stress]. The system opposes the stress by [mechanism], which [consequence to concentrations]."

Spontaneity: "The reaction is [spontaneous/nonspontaneous] because ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS° = [value] kJ, which is [negative/positive]."

IMFs: "[Molecule X] has a higher boiling point because it experiences [specific IMF], which requires more energy to overcome. [Molecule Y] only has [weaker IMF]."

Acid strength: "[Acid A] is stronger because [structural reason — bond strength, EN, resonance], making it more likely to donate a proton."

3
What graders actually look for
  • State symbols: (aq), (s), (g), (l) — check these every time
  • Specific chemical names/formulas, not vague words
  • Show math setup AND answer with units
  • If you make an error, keep going — subsequent steps can still earn points
  • Don't contradict yourself — a wrong claim that you then correct won't get you the point
  • Write legibly — graders can't give credit for what they can't read
Most common point losses: (1) Missing "because" — just stating a fact. (2) Unit errors in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS (J vs kJ). (3) Forgetting to convert °C to K. (4) Not showing ICE table setup. (5) Saying "equilibrium shifts right" without explaining what happens to concentrations as a result.
The 12-day rule: Every practice FRQ you write, cover your answer and ask: "Did I explain WHY, or did I just state WHAT?" If you only stated what, rewrite it. Do this consistently for 12 days and your FRQ score will improve more than anything else you can do.
